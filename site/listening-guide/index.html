Listening guide

Choose a sound by ear.

Start with the cue off at a comfortable volume. Compare each background with quiet in the room where you plan to use it.

Five backgrounds

Pink noise
A warm wash. Ideal pink noise puts equal energy in each octave, such as 250–500 Hz and 500–1,000 Hz.
White noise
A brighter, steadier hiss. Ideal white noise has equal power per hertz. Entrain rolls off the highs for a softer sound.
Soft rain
A synthetic shower. Fine hiss shifts gently above a steadier layer of noise throughout the session.
Wind
Soft gusts grow and settle, with a quieter rush moving through the stereo field.
Ocean surf
Swells gather, break into a brighter wash and recede. The waves vary throughout the session.

Noise spectra explained

Pitch, beats and cue timing

A 200 Hz tone vibrates 200 times per second. With stereo headphones, 200 Hz in one ear and 210 Hz in the other can produce a perceived 10 Hz binaural beat. Binaural-beat review.

A bilateral cue alternates between ears. At the default 625 ms interval, you hear 1.6 cues per second and return to the same ear after 1.25 seconds. Choose 350–2,000 ms between sounds. A longer interval slows the pace. Cue volume at 100% preserves the original bell, tone or noise burst. At 50% its amplitude is halved. At 0% only the background plays.

Entrain generates the audio pattern. Clinical EMDR is a structured psychotherapy guided by a clinician, with bilateral stimulation as one component. VA overview.

What the band names mean

EEG measures electrical activity at the scalp. A common convention calls frequencies below 4 Hz delta, 4 to below 8 theta, 8–13 alpha, 14–30 beta, and above 30 through 80 gamma. Boundaries vary by convention. Clinical EEG glossary.

A preset labeled “alpha” tells you its beat frequency. Measuring a listener’s EEG is a separate experiment, and studies disagree on whether binaural beats reliably change those rhythms. Research review.

What a background may help with

A steady bed can make nearby conversation or traffic less noticeable. Try the same task with and without it and listen for whether the interruptions stand out less. Effects on attention vary across listeners. Auditory masking review · Attention studies.

Evidence for better sleep from continuous noise is weak. A laboratory study of 25 healthy adults found less REM sleep with continuous pink noise than with quiet. Sleep review · Sleep laboratory study.
